# 05.02. Navigation and search

## Objectives

By the end of the lesson, students will be able to explain which questions navigation must answer, when search offers real value, and how to align results, filters, and no-results states with the user task.

## Orientation within the system

> [!note] Key idea
> Good navigation continuously helps answer three questions: where am I, what is here, and where can I go next? Breadcrumbs and menus are not the only answers. Page titles, selected states, content structure, and the ability to go back create orientation together.

Primary navigation should show frequent, high-value entry points. Secondary navigation can accommodate less frequent but necessary details. Do not try to make every piece of content equally visible in one enormous menu. Navigation supports the next meaningful decision rather than presenting the entire system structure.

## When is search needed?

Search is particularly useful when there is a large amount of distinct content, users seek a known name or term, or several equally important routes exist. It does not fix poor labeling. If users do not know what the thing they seek is called, a search field alone is insufficient; categories, examples, recommendations, or question-based entry points may help.

Design what happens during typing, with misspellings, with too many results, and with none. Effective filters reflect decision criteria users actually use. For event discovery, date, location, price, and accessibility may be relevant; an internal database category may be less useful.

## Results lists and filters

A result should show enough information for users to decide whether it is worth opening. A title alone is rarely enough. Show active search or filter conditions and make them easy to remove. If results are personalized or sorted, communicate that too: hidden logic can undermine trust.

## Worked example

A library search returns no results for “psych.” A poor state simply says “No results.” A better version preserves the query, suggests a similar search, offers to clear filters, and shows whom to contact for specialized needs. It does not invent false results, but it does not leave users at a dead end either.

## Common misconceptions

| Statement | Clarification |
| --- | --- |
| “If there is search, good navigation is unnecessary.” | Search helps with known goals; discovery and learning terminology also require structure. |
| “More filters produce more precise results.” | Too many or poorly understood filters increase effort and may hide good results. |

## Review questions

1. Where am I, why am I here, and where next: how does your project's navigation answer these?
2. In which task would users search for a known name, and when would they browse?
3. What would your results list show alongside the title?
4. What next step would the no-results state provide?

## Glossary

**Primary navigation:** navigation showing the system's most important entry points.  
**Filter:** a control for narrowing results by one or more properties.  
**No-results state:** a search outcome with no content matching the conditions.  
**Orientation:** users' understanding of their location and possible next steps.
