# 05.01. Content, labels, and mental models

## Objectives

Students will understand that information architecture is a system of content, labels, and decision paths aligned with user tasks, rather than a list of menu items. They will distinguish organizational terminology from user language and use simple methods to check whether a category or label is understandable.

## How do users look for information?

> [!note] Key idea
> People do not see a system's internal structure. They start with a goal, a situation, their own words, and previous experience. On a university website, a structure based on “case types” may make sense to administrators, while students arrive asking “how do I request an extension?” or “when must I upload my supporting document?” If navigation uses only internal process names, users do not know which door to enter.

A **mental model** is a person's understanding of how a system works. It may not be technically accurate, yet it determines expectations about labels, icons, and steps. The designer's task is to create an understandable connection between users' models and the system's actual rules, rather than hide every internal operation.

## Labels as promises

A label promises what content or action will follow a click. “More” and “Information” say little by themselves. “Change booking,” “Admission requirements,” and “Today's events” are more specific. Good labels are short, use the audience's language, and cannot be confused with another concept in context.

To check a label, ask participants what they expect behind it. Give them several content items and ask them to group them and name the groups. This card sorting exercise can reveal unexpected terms or categories even with a small sample. We are looking for signals of differences between the designer's logic and the user's, rather than a final truth.

## Hierarchy and priority

Not all content is equally important. Information architecture determines what belongs at the first level, what appears only when needed, and which details support decisions. An overly flat structure offers many equally weighted choices; an overly deep structure hides the route to the goal. Appropriate depth depends on how often, with how much certainty, and with what consequences people seek the information.

On an event page, date, location, price, and registration requirements are often primary decision information. The organizer's full history or archived news may be secondary. The arrangement serves the current task rather than judging the content's value.

## Worked example

A course registration page originally has menu items labeled “Course catalog,” “Curriculum structure,” “Administration,” and “Central services.” In interviews, students say “I'm looking for a course that doesn't clash” or “I want to check whether I meet the prerequisite.” Improvement involves more than renaming: bring forward starting points for the task, such as “Find a course,” “Timetable and clashes,” and “Prerequisites.” Internal categories can still exist in the background without directing users' first steps.

## Common misconceptions

| Statement | Clarification |
| --- | --- |
| “The precise official name is always the best label.” | If the audience does not know it, a plain-language name and additional explanation may be needed. |
| “More categories mean more detail, so they are better.” | The cost of choosing may increase if differences are unclear. |
| “A good label works the same everywhere.” | Context and user goals also influence meaning. |

## Review questions

1. Which label could a new user of your project misunderstand?
2. Which internal organizational category does not match the user's question?
3. Which decision information should you bring forward?
4. How would you check label clarity with a small sample?

## Glossary

**Information architecture:** organizing content, categories, labels, and navigation around tasks.  
**Label:** a name communicating the expected meaning of content or an action.  
**Mental model:** the user's understanding of how a system works.  
**Card sorting:** a research method in which users group content items.
