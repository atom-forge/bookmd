# Project brief and stakeholders

## Objectives

By the end of the lesson, students will be able to narrow the semester project to one manageable task, distinguish evidenced elements of the brief from assumptions, and map whom a decision affects beyond the end user.

## What is a brief for?

> [!note] Key idea
> A project brief is neither marketing copy nor a feature list. It records the situation you are investigating, why it matters, for whom the solution should create value, the boundaries of your work, and what you are **not** undertaking. Its purpose is to provide a reference when a new idea would expand the scope or a discussion distracts from the main task.

A weak brief says: “I will design an app for university administration.” This involves too many people, processes, and consequences. A more manageable brief is: “I will investigate how a first-year student can identify and submit the documents needed for a single request for missing documents, without searching several places for contradictory information.” The second version identifies the user, task, assumed obstacle, and designable boundary.

## The basic elements of a brief

For a semester project, a brief generally needs a short description of the problem area, target user, primary task, current situation, organizational or operational goal, known constraints, research questions, success criteria, and exclusions. Exclusions are especially important: for example, the project does not cover backend development, payment, or every possible user role.

For every statement, ask where it comes from. A brief may begin with client information, your own observations, or preliminary research, but do not conflate them. “Students do not understand the process” may be an assumption to investigate, while “the system contains three different forms” is a verifiable fact.

## Who are the stakeholders?

The primary user is the person whose main task you are designing for. But decisions affect other people too. In an administrative interface, stakeholders may include the administrator receiving incomplete applications, the department setting rules, IT operations maintaining the system, and the person a student turns to for help. A stakeholder map does not mean building a separate product for everyone. It means understanding who supplies data, who sets rules, who bears the consequences of errors, and who may later make decisions.

Create a table with the stakeholder, their goal or interest, their influence on the project, and an open question concerning them. This reveals conflicts early. Users want quick submission, while administrators want complete documentation. A good design need not choose one over the other: preliminary checks and clear requirements can reduce the burden on both.

## Scope and decision discipline

Narrowing scope enables learning rather than diminishing the problem. If you try to design everything in one prototype—search, account management, notifications, payment, and an admin interface—you will not research or test any decision meaningfully. Choose one high-value task with substantial uncertainty and define a clear beginning and end.

A useful question is: where would users lose the most if they made a mistake or gave up? This could be selecting the right service, checking information, or interpreting confirmation. A brief does not need to answer everything; it should identify which questions deserve research.

## Worked example

In a local sports court booking project, the initial idea is “search for and book courts.” Before research, however, we do not know whether users choose primarily by price, location, group size, or time. The brief could therefore narrow the focus to “selecting a suitable court and understanding booking conditions for someone organizing a group of friends.” It can exclude payment integration and management of the operator's calendar while identifying these as external constraints.

## Review questions

1. What single task can you research and test from beginning to end in your project?
2. Which brief statements are facts and which are assumptions?
3. Who would bear the greatest consequences if the main task failed?
4. What are you deliberately excluding from the project?

## Glossary

**Brief:** a document recording a project's problem, goals, boundaries, and open questions.  
**Stakeholder:** a person or organization affected by a service or design decision.  
**Scope:** the boundary between tasks undertaken and deliberately excluded.  
**Primary user:** the person whose main task the solution is optimized for.
