# Tasks, moderation, and observation

## Objectives

Students will be able to prepare neutral test tasks and a moderator script. They will distinguish participant behavior from their own interpretation and know when to observe quietly and when to ask follow-up questions without suggesting a solution.

## A task, not a route

> [!note] Key idea
> Task instructions provide a brief, plausible situation without naming controls or the desired click sequence. “You cannot attend your booking tomorrow. Find out what you can do” is a good task. “Find the cancellation menu item” already hides whether participants would recognize the option themselves.

Three to five tasks are usually enough for a test. Begin with an easier orientation task, then move toward the main goal and critical decisions. Do not connect tasks so that an earlier error makes a later one impossible; reset the prototype or provide a new starting point when necessary.

## The moderator's role

Moderators make the situation safe without helping solve the task. They can invite thinking aloud: “Tell me what you are looking for or expecting.” They can ask: “What do you think would happen if you selected this?” They should not say: “Try the menu in the top right.”

Getting stuck is data. Do not rescue participants too early and erase the problem. If the task stops progressing and must be interrupted because of time limits or emotional strain, briefly record the point, explain that it provides information about the design, and move on. At the end, ask whether any moments felt uncertain, but keep observation more important than retrospective explanations.

## Taking notes

A simple table helps: task, observation, exact quote, consequence, possible cause, and open question. “The navigation is poor” is not an observation. “The participant returned to the home page three times while looking for how to change a booking” is. A later interpretation might be “editing is not where users expect,” but label it as a hypothesis.

## Worked example

After booking, a participant cannot find the confirmation. Instead of pointing it out, the moderator asks: “Where would you expect it to be?” The participant says they would expect an email, then searches their profile. The finding is that the system did not clearly communicate the channel and location of confirmation, rather than simply that the participant did not find a button.

## Review questions

1. Does your test task name the goal or the control?
2. What sentence would you use to invite thinking aloud?
3. When would you interrupt a participant who is stuck?
4. Write an observation and a separate interpretation.

## Glossary

**Moderation:** facilitating the test and supporting participants without suggesting the solution.  
**Think-aloud:** participants voicing their expectations and interpretations during a task.  
**Observation:** a directly recordable action or quote.  
**Interpretation:** a possible cause or design implication of an observation.
