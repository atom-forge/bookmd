# Test goals, participants, and ethics

## Objectives

Students will be able to plan a usability test with a clear learning goal, select participants relevant to the task, and conduct testing while respecting participants' safety, time, and dignity.

## What does a usability test investigate?

> [!note] Key idea
> A usability test is not an opinion poll or a check of whether people “like” the design. We observe how someone tries to achieve a realistic goal in the design: what they understand, expect, find uncertain, which detours they take, and whether they reach the intended outcome. Before testing, formulate one or two learning goals. For example: “Do participants understand when the booking becomes final?” or “Is the information needed to choose a suitable appointment visible where the decision happens?”

An overly broad goal—“test the app”—produces poor notes because you do not know why each observation matters. An overly narrow goal—“do they find the blue button?”—checks only a solution rather than the user task. Express the goal in terms of the problem and hypothesis.

## Who should participate?

You do not need a perfectly representative sample, but participants should resemble target users in ways relevant to the task. If you design for people handling an administrative process for the first time, do not test only experienced system users. If the device or environment matters, such as mobile use while traveling, consider that too.

In a small student project, three to five relevant tests can reveal many problems, especially when the same error recurs. This is not statistical proof. Report “three of the participants studied got stuck,” rather than “all users will do this.”

## Consent and safety

Even for a short test, explain what will happen, what notes will be taken, whether there will be a recording, and that participants can stop at any time. Emphasize that you are testing the design, not them. Do not request real passwords, personal identifiers, banking details, or information unnecessary for the task. Record only with explicit permission, and define the retention period.

Do not promise rewards or benefits that pressure people to participate. Their feedback is valuable, but they are not obliged to be “useful” or give positive opinions.

## Worked example

A student tests a university application submission process. Learning goal: do participants recognize the required documents and know whether submission succeeded? Participant: a student who has handled administration online but does not routinely use this particular application. Before testing, the facilitator says: “There are no right or wrong answers; I want to understand the design. We can take a break at any time.” They then provide a situation rather than a list of features.

## Common misconceptions

| Statement | Clarification |
| --- | --- |
| “My friends are sufficient participants.” | They may be relevant if their circumstances match the task; goodwill does not replace relevance. |
| “Three tests produce a certain decision.” | Three tests can provide strong signals, but uncertainty and sample limitations must be documented. |
| “Consent is merely a legal formality.” | It underpins trust and safety, which also enables better, more honest observations. |

## Review questions

1. What two specific things do you want to learn from your test?
2. What would make a participant relevant to your project?
3. What information must you not request during testing?
4. How would you describe the sample's limitations in your report?

## Glossary

**Usability test:** observing users performing tasks in a product or prototype.  
**Test goal:** a question defined in advance that the test helps answer.  
**Participant:** a person performing tasks in a test who is relevant to the target user.  
**Consent:** informed, voluntary agreement to participation and data handling.
