# Interviews and observation

## Objectives

Students will be able to conduct a brief, ethical, non-leading research conversation; distinguish participant quotes, observable behavior, and their own interpretations; and explain why questions about specific past situations matter.

## A conversation, not an interrogation

> [!note] Key idea
> A good research interview does not aim to get the “right” answer or make participants like the designer's idea. Its purpose is to understand a real situation. Begin with a short introduction: explain that you are investigating the process, will treat answers confidentially, and that participants may skip any question. Do not promise a feature or solution that has not been decided on.

Ask for past examples rather than general opinions. After “Do you book appointments online?”, ask: “Could you tell me about the last time? What prompted it? What did you do first? Where were you uncertain?” Details—open tabs, screenshots, a friend they asked, an abandoned step—often reveal more than an overall judgment.

## Questioning techniques

Use open questions: “What happened?”, “How did you decide?”, “What would you have expected here?” A bare “why?” can sometimes sound accusatory, so “What mattered to you here?” or “What did you base that choice on?” is often better. Follow interesting details, but do not argue, correct participants, or start explaining the system.

Avoid leading questions. “Was it annoying that cancellation was so hidden?” already tells participants what they should feel. A neutral version is: “If you wanted to change the booking, where would you look?” Also avoid combining two questions: “Was it easy and quick?” Participants may feel yes about one and no about the other.

## Observation and thinking aloud

When participants demonstrate a real task, observe their actions. Which information do they look for first? What do they click before deciding? When do they pause or go back? With the think-aloud method, you can ask them to describe their expectations and interpretations, but do not demand continuous speech: the task and observation matter more.

Distinguish three types of notes. **Quote:** “I don't know whether this is final now.” **Observation:** the participant returned to the summary page twice. **Interpretation:** the confirmation wording did not provide enough reassurance. The last is a hypothesis, not a fact; further data must later support or reject it.

## Ethics and data handling

Do not request unnecessary personal, health, financial, or other sensitive information. During screen sharing, participants may stop at any time, and you must not ask them to show real passwords or private messages. Even for brief notes, indicate who has access and how long you will retain them. Participants' time and trust are resources, not free testing material.

## Worked example

A participant describes searching for a university scholarship. Instead of asking “Would a filter help?”, ask them to show their most recent search. You may discover that they gave up because the eligibility conditions were difficult to interpret, rather than because there were too many results. The design implication is not automatically a filter: it might be a clear explanation of conditions, eligibility screening questions, or a route to request help.

## Review questions

1. Why is asking about a past example more useful than asking about future intentions?
2. Rewrite a leading question as a neutral question.
3. Which is a fact and which an interpretation: “The participant searched for two minutes”; “the navigation is poor”?
4. What personal information is unnecessary to collect for your project?

## Glossary

**Semi-structured interview:** a conversation based on predetermined topics while allowing follow-up exploration.  
**Think-aloud:** a method in which participants voice their expectations and interpretations during a task.  
**Leading question:** a question that suggests a desired answer.  
**Observation:** directly recordable behavior or an event, rather than its explanation.
