# Research questions and method selection

## Objectives

By the end of this lesson, students will be able to turn design uncertainty into a research question, choose a proportionate research method, and recognize when a finding provides behavioral evidence and when it provides only an opinion or assumption.

## The purpose of research: reducing uncertainty

> [!note] Key idea
> User research is not good simply because we ask many questions or produce an attractive chart at the end. Its purpose is to enable a decision for which we previously lacked sufficient knowledge. A research question therefore always connects to a design risk.

Suppose we are designing an appointment booking service. “Would people like a new app?” is not useful, because it invites a polite yes. Better questions are: “How do users currently find a suitable appointment?”, “What information do they use to choose?”, “Where do they become uncertain?”, and “What happens if they cannot find a suitable option?” The answers can inform decisions about structure, content, and interaction.

A good question is narrow enough to investigate without suggesting the answer. “Why don't they use the calendar view?” already assumes the calendar view is the problem. “How do they compare available appointments?” leaves room for discovery.

## Exploratory, evaluative, and measurement questions

We can distinguish three common types of question according to the purpose of the research.

**Exploratory research** is needed when we do not yet understand the situation. We investigate goals, habits, obstacles, language, and workarounds. Suitable methods include semi-structured interviews, contextual observation, or analysis of existing customer support feedback.

**Evaluative research** is needed once a design or prototype exists. We investigate whether users achieve their goal, understand the content, and find the function. Examples include usability testing, tree testing, and working through a clickable prototype.

**Measurement research** investigates how common something is or compares two options. Surveys, analytics, or experiments can help, but only if the sample and measurement approach fit the question. Three conversations cannot support percentage claims about a population; however, three similar difficulties can strongly indicate a needed prototype improvement.

## Choose the method to fit the question

Interviews help us understand past situations, motivations, and users' own words. Observation reveals what people actually do during a task, which often differs from their later account. Surveys can test known patterns with larger samples, but are poor at uncovering new problems because we must devise the questions in advance. A usability test does not tell us whether the product is needed; it tells us whether the chosen solution works for the task.

Research time is often the biggest constraint in a small student project. In that case, refine the question rather than increase the number of methods. Three relevant, well-documented conversations can be more valuable than fifty survey responses that only measure whether people like an idea in principle.

## A minimal research plan

Prepare a one-page plan even for brief research. Include:

1. The decision requiring more knowledge.
2. The research questions and their associated assumptions.
3. A description of participants relevant to the task.
4. The method, time frame, and data handling approach.
5. What counts as sufficient evidence for the next design step.

This is not bureaucracy. It helps prevent an interview from suddenly becoming a product demonstration or a later decision from resting solely on the most memorable quote.

## Worked example

A student is designing a community donation interface. Their first idea is that donors need detailed project descriptions. However, the research question could be: “What information do people use to decide whether to support a local cause?” Interviews may show that participants look for a specific goal, organizational credibility, and the expected impact of their donation, rather than a long story. The next design step is therefore to structure comparable information that provides evidence, rather than simply write a longer description.

## Common misconceptions

| Statement | Clarification |
| --- | --- |
| “Research should validate my idea.” | Research is useful when it can also disprove or change an assumption. |
| “Surveys are objective; interviews are subjective.” | Every method has biases. Question wording and respondent selection can affect surveys; interviewer behavior can affect interviews. |
| “You cannot learn anything from a small sample.” | You cannot estimate general proportions, but you can identify recurring obstacles within a task. |

## Review questions

1. Which design decision still lacks sufficient evidence?
2. Why is “Would you use an app like this?” a poor research question?
3. Which method would best reveal the current workaround in your project?
4. What would make your research proportionate to the available time?

## Glossary

**Exploratory research:** research aimed at understanding a situation, goals, and problems.  
**Evaluative research:** investigating the usability of an existing solution or prototype.  
**Research question:** an open question whose answer influences a design decision.  
**Methodology:** the procedure and set of rules chosen to answer a research question.
