# Hierarchy, layout, and copy

## Objectives

Students will be able to create visual and content hierarchy around the user task. They will understand that size, position, white space, contrast, grouping, and microcopy guide attention together, and that making a button larger is only one hierarchy tool.

## Designing attention

> [!note] Key idea
> Every screen element competes for attention. If every heading is large, every button brightly colored, and every piece of information emphasized, nothing has clear priority. Hierarchy communicates what to notice first, what to understand before deciding, and what can remain secondary or available only when needed.

Its tools include size, weight, color contrast, placement, repetition, white space, and proximity between related elements. However, visual decisions need content-based reasons. When choosing an appointment, the current selection, critical condition, and next action should stand out, rather than necessarily the decorative image.

## Layout and grouping

Users interpret nearby, similarly formatted elements as related. Use this deliberately: keep a field's label close to the field and a price condition near the price rather than in a distant footnote. Leave enough space between groups to reveal the structure, but avoid dividing the screen into too many boxes.

Layout should preserve its logic when screen size changes. On mobile, we do more than compress the desktop view: we decide the order in which users receive information, what remains visible, and what can be opened when needed.

## Microcopy

Short text—buttons, labels, help text, and error messages—is part of the task. “Continue” works only if the context is clear. “Review booking” or “Submit application” names the consequence even on its own. Labels should not simply reflect internal system states when users arrive with a different goal.

For critical actions, wording can reduce risk. Alongside “Finalize,” clearly state what will become final. Put the explanation where the decision happens rather than hiding it in separate help.

## Worked example

A form places an equally large “Cancel” button, three links, and an advertisement-like information panel beside the primary “Submit” button. Participants are unsure which step completes the process. The improvement does not necessarily require more color: give the main action a clear label and distinct location; keep cancellation available but less prominent; move supporting information near the relevant field or into a secondary layer.

## Common misconceptions

| Statement | Clarification |
| --- | --- |
| “Hierarchy is purely visual style.” | Content order and wording also create hierarchy. |
| “Shorter text is always better.” | Labels that are too short and vague increase uncertainty. |

## Review questions

1. What should users notice first on the main screen?
2. Which elements currently seem equally important without justification?
3. Is your main button's label understandable on its own?
4. Which information have you placed too far from the related decision?

## Glossary

**Visual hierarchy:** arranging attention and information priority through visual means.  
**Microcopy:** short interface text supporting a task.  
**Grouping:** the spatial and visual organization of related information.  
**White space:** deliberately unoccupied space that separates elements and creates focus.
